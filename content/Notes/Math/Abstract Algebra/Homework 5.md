$$
D_{2n}= \langle r, s | r^n = e, s^2 =e, s r=r^{-1}s \rangle
$$

> [!question]- For any $x \in D_{2n}$ show that either $x = r^k$ for $k \in \mathbb{N}$ or $x^{-1}rx=r^{-1}$. 
> $r^n=e$ so every $x \in D_{2n}$ is either of the form $x = r^k$ or $x=r^ks$ for some $k \in \{ 1, 2, \cdots, n \}$. If $x = r^k$ we are done, otherwise $x = r^ks$. $s^{-1}=s$ so $x^{-1}=s^{-1}r^{-k}=s r^{-k}$, thus:
> 
>$$
> \begin{align}
> x^{-1}rx & = s r^{-k}rr^ks \\
>  & = s r s \\
>   & = r^{-1}ss \\
>    & = r^{-1}
> \end{align}
>$$


> [!question]- Show that any $t \in D_{2n}$ that is not a power of $r$ must be of order 2. Prove that $D_{2n}$ is generated by $s$ and $sr$. 
> $t$ is not a power of $r$, so by the first question we then have that $t=r^ks$. Then:
>$$
> \begin{align}
> t^2  & = r^k s r^k s  \\
>  & = r^k (s r^k s) \\
>   & = r^k (s r s)^k   \\
>    & = r^k (r^{-1}s s)^k \\
>	 &  = r^k r^{-k} \\
>	  & = e
> \end{align}
>$$
> Also $t \ne e$ since $r^n=e$ is a power of $r$. Then, since $s, s r \in D_{2n}$ and $D_{2n}$ is closed so $\langle s, s r \rangle \subseteq D_{2n}$. Also, $s \in \langle s, s r \rangle$ and $s(s r)=r \in \langle s, s r \rangle$ so by closure $r^k$ and $r^ks$ are also in $\langle s, s r \rangle$. By the first question, every element of $D_{2n}$ is of the form $r^k$ or $r^ks$ so $D_{2n} \subseteq \langle s, s r \rangle$. Therefore, $D_{2n} = \langle s, s r \rangle$.


> [!question]- Prove that the centre of $D_{2n}$ is trivial if $n \ne 1$ is odd. 
>  Let $n \ne 1$ be odd. $e \in Z(D_{2n})$ but let some other element $z \in Z(D_{2n})$.  I will show that $z = e$. $z$ is in the centre so $zr = rz$ thus $z^{-1}rz=z^{-1}zr=r$. If $z$ is not a power of $r$, then $z^{-1}rz=r^{-1}$ so $r = r^{-1}$ meaning $r^2=e$ but $r^n=e$ yet we assumed $n$ is odd so this is contradiction. This means that $z=r^k$ for some $k \in \{ 0, 1, \cdots, n \}$ but $z$ also commutes with $s$ so $szs=zss=z$, above we showed that $s r^k s = r^{-k}$ so $r^k=r^{-k}$ and $r^{2k}= e$. $|r|=n$ so $n | 2k$, $n$ is odd so $n$ must divide $k$, since $1 \leq k\leq n$ we have that $k=n$ and $z = r^n = e$. So the centre is trivial.


> [!question]- Show that the function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ defined $f(x)=3x$ is a bijective homomorphism (of additive groups) 
> Let $x,y \in \mathbb{R}$ then $f(x+y)=3(x+y)=3x +3y =f(x) + f(y)$ so $f$ is a homomorphism. If $f(x)=f(y)$ then $3x=3y$ so $x=y$ and hence $f$ is injective. Also, for any $y \in \mathbb{R}$, $\frac{y}{3} \in \mathbb{R}$ and $f\left( \frac{y}{3} \right)=y$ so $f$ is surjetive. $f$ is a hom, injective, and surjective, thus it is a bijective hom and is an isomorphism.
>

> [!question]- Let $f: G \to H$ be a isomorphism. Prove that for all $g \in G$, $|g|=|f(g)|$ 
> $f(e_{G})=f(e_{G}e_{G})=f(e_{G})f(e_{G})$, so multiplying by $f(e_{G})^{-1}$ yields $f(e_{G})=e_{H}$. Also $f$ is a hom so $f(g^m)=f(g)^m$ for all $m\geq 1$. Now let $m\geq 1$ if $g^m = e_{G}$ then $f(g^m)=f(g)^m=f(e_{G})=e_{H}$. Conversely, if $f(g)^m = e_{H}$ then $f(g^m)=e_{H}=f(e_{G})$ and $g^m=e^G$ as $f$ is injective. So $g^m=e_{G} \iff f(g)^m = e_{H}$ for all $m\geq 1$. Hence the least such $m$ is the same for $g$ and $f(g)$.


> [!question]- If $K \leq G$ then $f(K)\leq H$ ($f$ is a hom from $G \to H$)
> Let $K \leq G$, $f(K) \subseteq H$. Let $a, b \in K$ then $ab \in K$ and $f(a)f(b)=f(ab) \in f(K)$. The operation of $H$ is associative so it is also associative on $f(K)$. $e_{G} \in K$ so $f(e_{G})=e_{H} \in f(K)$. Finally, let $a \in K$ then $a^{-1} \in K$ so $f(aa^{-1})=f(a)f(a^{-1})=f(e_{G})=e_{H}$ so $f(K)$ is closed under inverses and $f(K) \leq H$.


> [!question]- If $f: G \to H$ is an isomorphism and $T\leq G$, prove that $T$ is isomorphic to $f(T)=\{ f(a) | a \in T \}$.  
> From the previous question we know that $f(T)$ is a subgroup of $H$. Now consider $g: T \to f(T)$  where $g(a)=f(a)$. For $a, b \in T$ we have $g(ab)=f(ab)=f(a)f(b)=g(a)g(b)$ so $g$ is an homomorphism. $g$ is injective because $f$ is, also $g$ is surjective because every element of $f(T)$ $f(a)=g(a)$ for some $a \in T$. Thus $g$ is an isomorphism and $T \simeq f(T)$.


> [!question]- Let $a \in G$, prove that $a^{-1}Ta= \{ a^{-1}ta: t \in T \}$ is a subgroup of $G$ isomorphic to $T$. 
> Consider $c: G \to G$ defined $c(x)=a^{-1}xa$ for $x, y \in G$, then:
>$$
> \begin{align}
> c(x)c(y) & =a^{-1}xa a^{-1}ya \\
>  & = a^{-1}xya \\
>   & = c(xy)
> \end{align} 
>$$
>So, $c$ is an homomorphism. Furthermore, $c$ is a bijection with inverse $axa^{-1}$ so $c$ is an isomorphism and $c(T)=a^{-1}Ta\leq G$ by a previous question, and we can conclude that $T \simeq c(T)=a^{-1}Ta$.

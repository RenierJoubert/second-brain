# Mechanical Vibrations

Mechanical vibrations are really just applications of second order constant coefficient linear equations.

For example, suppose we have some mass $m$ on a spring, its motion over time can be modeled by the *mass-spring-damper system*:
$$
mx'' + cx' + kx = F(t)
$$
Where $c$ is the damping constant, $k$ is the spring constant and $F(t)$ is some external force. The motion is considered *forced* (or *driven*) if $F \neq 0$ and *free* if $F=0$. Similarly, it is *damped* if $c>0$ and *undamped* if $c=0$.

## Free Undamped Motion

Free undamped motion is just modeled by:
$$
mx'' + kx = 0
$$
Where the general solution is of the form:
$$
x(t) = A\cos(\omega_{0}t) + B\sin(\omega_{0}t)
$$
And $\omega_{0} = \sqrt{ \frac{k}{m} }$ is called the *natural frequency* of the system. Since there is no damping, the amplitude of the motion stays constant.

## Free Damped Motion

Now, free damped motion is modeled by the homogeneous equation:
$$
mx'' + cx' + kx = 0
$$
Where dividing by $m$ we can write:
$$
x'' + 2px' + \omega_{0}^2x = 0
$$
and $p = \frac{c}{2m}$. Which we can solve using the same method in [[2.2 Solving Constant Coefficient 2nd Order ODEs]], where the roots are $r = -p \pm \sqrt{ p^2-\omega_{0}^2 }$.

So, when $c^2-4km>0$ the system is *overdamped* and there are two distinct real roots such that:
$$
x(t) = C_{1}e^{ r_{1}t } + C_{2}e^{ r_{2}t }
$$
When $c^2-4km=0$ the system is *critically damped* and there is one real root of multiplicity 2, namely $r=-p$, such that:
$$
x(t) = (C_{1} + C_{2}t)e^{ -pt }
$$
Whereas if $c^2-4km<0$ the system is *underdamped* and there are complex roots $r = -p \pm i\omega_{d}$ such that:
$$
x(t) = e^{ -pt }(C_{1}\cos(\omega_{d}t) + C_{2}\sin(\omega_{d}t))
$$
Where $\omega_{d} = \sqrt{ \omega_{0}^2-p^2 }$ is called the *damped natural frequency*.

In brief, only the underdamped case oscillates, and its amplitude decays exponentially as energy is lost over time. In the overdamped and critically damped cases the mass returns to equilibrium without oscillating.

## The Friction Constant

Rearranging $\omega_{d} = \sqrt{ \omega_{0}^2-p^2 }$ with $p = \frac{c}{2m}$, we can find the friction constant from the two frequencies:
$$
c = 2m\sqrt{ \omega_{0}^2-\omega_{d}^2 }
$$
Where $\omega_{0}$ is the natural frequency and $\omega_{d}$ is the damped natural frequency.

## Forced Vibrations

For a forced system of the form:
$$
mx'' + cx' + kx = F(t)
$$
We can find the general solution using the method from [[2.5 Non-homogeneous Equations]] such that:
$$
x(t) = x_{c}(t) + x_{p}(t)
$$
Where if $c>0$, $x_c$ dies out over time so it is called the *transient solution*, and $x_p$ is called the *steady-state solution*.

If the forcing is sinusoidal, $F(t) = F_{0}\cos(\omega t)$, this also leads into *resonance*. Namely, if there is no damping and $\omega=\omega_{0}$ then our guess for $x_p$ solves the homogeneous equation, so just like the edge cases in [[2.5 Non-homogeneous Equations]] we multiply it by $t$ and the amplitude grows without bound.

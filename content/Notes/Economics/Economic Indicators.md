# The 5 Key Economic Indicators

Economic indicators are statistics that allow analysts, investors, and policymakers to assess the health of an economy and anticipate future conditions. They are typically classified as:

- **Leading indicators** — change _before_ the economy shifts (forward-looking signal)
- **Lagging indicators** — change _after_ the economy shifts (confirmation)
- **Coincident indicators** — change _at the same time_ as the economy

## 1. Economic Growth

**GDP (Gross Domestic Product)** is the market value of all goods and services produced within a country over a given period. It is the broadest and most comprehensive barometer of economic activity.

### The GDP Formula

$$GDP = C + I + G + (X - M)$$

|Variable|Meaning|
|---|---|
|**C**|Personal consumption (household spending)|
|**I**|Private investment (business capex, residential investment)|
|**G**|Government consumption and investment|
|**X**|Exports|
|**M**|Imports|

> **(X − M)** is the **net trade balance** — a trade surplus adds to GDP; a trade deficit subtracts from it.

### Nominal vs. Real GDP

**Nominal GDP** is measured in current dollar terms and is _not_ a reliable measure of true economic growth because it is distorted by inflation. A rise in nominal GDP could simply reflect rising prices rather than genuine output growth.

**Real GDP** strips out the effects of inflation:

$$ \text{Real GDP Growth (\%)} = \text{Nominal GDP Growth (\%)} - \text{Inflation (\%)} $$

> **Example:** If nominal GDP grows 6% but inflation is 4%, real GDP growth is only 2%.

### GDP Reporting

- Published **quarterly** by the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) in the U.S.
- Three estimates are released: **Advance** (first estimate, ~1 month after quarter ends) → **Second** → **Third (Final)**
- Markets typically react most strongly to the Advance estimate

### Recession

> A **recession** is conventionally defined as **two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth**.

- More formally, the NBER (National Bureau of Economic Research) in the U.S. considers a broader set of indicators including employment, income, and industrial production
- A severe, prolonged recession is called a **depression**
- GDP growth below trend but still positive is sometimes called a **slowdown** or **soft patch**

## 2. Inflation

**Inflation** is the general, sustained increase in the prices of goods and services across an economy, which erodes the purchasing power of money over time.

$$\text{Purchasing Power} \downarrow \quad \text{as} \quad \text{Price Level} \uparrow$$

**Deflation** (falling prices) can be equally damaging however as it discourages spending and investment as consumers and businesses wait for prices to fall further, potentially triggering a deflationary spiral.

### Sources of Inflation

|Type|Description|Example|
|---|---|---|
|**Demand-pull**|Demand exceeds supply ("too much money chasing too few goods")|Post-COVID stimulus boom|
|**Cost-push**|Rising input costs passed on to consumers|Oil price shocks|
|**Built-in (wage-price spiral)**|Higher wages → higher costs → higher prices → repeat|1970s stagflation|
|**Monetary**|Excessive money supply growth|Hyperinflation in Zimbabwe, Weimar Germany|

### Key Inflation Measures

#### 1. CPI — Consumer Price Index

- Tracks the price change of a fixed **basket of goods and services** purchased by a typical urban consumer
- Published **monthly** by the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS)
- **Core CPI** excludes food and energy (more volatile components) to show underlying trend
- For CPI to be truly representative, the basket must reflect the actual spending habits of the population it measures — this composition varies by country

#### 2. PCE — Personal Consumption Expenditures

- Published **monthly** as part of the BEA's Personal Income and Outlays report
- The **Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge**
- Broader than CPI — covers all goods and services consumed, not just out-of-pocket expenses
- Uses a **chain-weighted formula** that adjusts for substitution behaviour (e.g. if beef prices rise, consumers switch to chicken — PCE captures this, CPI does not)
- **Core PCE** (ex-food and energy) is the Fed's primary target metric; the target is **2%**

#### 3. PPI — Producer Price Index

- Measures price changes at the **wholesale/producer level**, before goods reach consumers
- Acts as a **leading indicator** for future CPI — producer cost increases are often passed downstream

### Why Inflation Matters to Markets

- Central banks raise interest rates to combat high inflation → higher borrowing costs → slows growth
- Fixed income assets (bonds) lose value in high inflation environments
- Equities react depending on whether inflation is driven by demand (often positive) or costs (often negative for margins)

## 3. Unemployment

Consumers make up more than **two-thirds of the U.S. economy**, and household spending is fundamentally driven by employment income. A sustained rise in unemployment depresses consumer spending, drags on GDP, and is a critical political and social statistic.

### Key Unemployment Measures

#### Nonfarm Payrolls (NFP)

- Measures the **monthly change in the number of employed persons** in the U.S., excluding farm workers and a handful of other categories (private household employees, non-profit employees, government workers in some contexts)
- Released the **first Friday of each month** by the BLS
- One of the most market-moving data releases globally — large deviations from expectations can cause significant moves in equities, bonds, and FX

#### Unemployment Rate

- The percentage of the **labour force** that is jobless and actively seeking work
- Published alongside NFP in the BLS Jobs Report
- **U-3** is the headline rate; **U-6** (broader measure) includes discouraged workers and those in part-time work for economic reasons — considered a better gauge of true labour market slack

#### Initial Jobless Claims

- Weekly count of new unemployment insurance filings
- A **leading indicator** — spikes signal deteriorating labour market conditions in real time

### The Fed's Dual Mandate

The U.S. Federal Reserve targets both **price stability (2% inflation)** and **maximum employment** — these two objectives sometimes conflict, forcing trade-offs in monetary policy.

## 4. Business Confidence

### PMI — Purchasing Managers' Index

The PMI is a **leading indicator** of economic activity, derived from monthly surveys of purchasing managers across manufacturing and services sectors.

- A reading **above 50** = expansion
- A reading **below 50** = contraction
- The speed of change matters — a PMI falling from 58 to 52 signals slowing growth even if still expansionary

### Components of the Manufacturing PMI

Surveyed areas include: new orders, output, employment, supplier delivery times, and inventory levels.

### Types of PMI

|PMI|Coverage|
|---|---|
|**Manufacturing PMI**|Factory output, orders, inventories|
|**Services PMI**|Service sector activity (the larger share of most developed economies)|
|**Composite PMI**|Weighted blend of both|

### Key PMI Publishers

- **S&P Global / Markit** — global coverage
- **ISM (Institute for Supply Management)** — U.S.-focused, widely followed by markets

### Other Confidence Indicators

- **Consumer Confidence Index (CCI)** — tracks household sentiment on current and future economic conditions
- **IFO Business Climate Index** — key gauge for the German/European economy
- **ZEW Economic Sentiment** — forward-looking survey of financial analysts

## 5. Housing

Home building directly accounts for only ~**3% of U.S. GDP**, yet housing is a powerful **leading economic indicator** because of its broad second-order effects.

### Why Housing Leads the Economy

Before a home is built, developers must believe consumers are confident enough to take on mortgages — making housing starts a signal of forward-looking consumer and credit confidence. After purchase, homeowners generate significant downstream economic activity:

- Furniture, appliances, and home goods
- Landscaping and renovation services
- Financial services (mortgages, insurance)
- Utilities and ongoing maintenance

This multiplier effect means housing's true economic footprint far exceeds its direct 3% share of GDP.

### Key Housing Indicators

|Indicator|What It Measures|
|---|---|
|**Housing Starts**|Number of new residential construction projects begun — leading indicator|
|**Building Permits**|Authorizations for future construction — even more forward-looking than starts|
|**Existing Home Sales**|Volume of previously owned homes sold; reflects consumer confidence and credit availability|
|**New Home Sales**|Sales of newly constructed homes; more volatile, smaller sample|
|**Case-Shiller Home Price Index**|Tracks changes in residential real estate prices across 20 major U.S. cities|
|**30-Year Mortgage Rate**|Heavily influenced by the 10-year Treasury yield; a key driver of housing affordability|

### Housing & Monetary Policy

Housing is particularly sensitive to interest rates because most purchases are debt-financed. When the Fed raises rates, mortgage rates rise, affordability falls, and housing activity slows — making housing one of the fastest sectors to feel the impact of tighter monetary policy.

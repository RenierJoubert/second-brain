# Currency

**Currency** is a medium of exchange for goods and services that eliminates the need for bartering, enabling price comparison across an economy. Currency only holds value as long as its users believe it will retain that value and this trust is the foundation of any monetary system.

---

## Why Doesn't the World Use a Single Currency?

Currency is a tool of economic control. Each country requires its own currency to most accurately represent the value of goods and services within its economy. A single global currency would eliminate the ability to use exchange rates and monetary policy as economic levers.

When multiple countries adopt a single currency, it can drive **economic convergence** wherein weaker nations tend to improve their economic performance following adoption (e.g. the Eurozone).

**Trade-offs of currency union:**

-  Eliminates exchange rate risk between members
-  Reduces transaction costs for cross-border trade
-  Surrenders independent monetary policy
-  Cannot devalue currency to regain competitiveness during downturns

## Types of Currencies

### Global Reserve Currency

The currency most widely held by central banks and used to price global commodities and international trade.

- **USD**: the world's dominant reserve currency since the Bretton Woods Agreement (1944)
- Approx. 60% of global foreign exchange reserves are held in USD

### Major Floating Currencies (G10)

Currencies of the world's leading industrialized economies whose values float freely on the open market. High liquidity and minimal restrictions on conversion.

|NZD|New Zealand|
|NOK|Norway|
|SEK|Sweden|

### Emerging & Restricted Currencies

Some currencies carry limitations or restrictions on conversion and liquidity, making them less freely tradable.

- **Chinese Yuan (CNY/RMB)** — managed by the PBOC within a controlled band
- **Russian Ruble (RUB)** — subject to capital controls, especially post-2022 sanctions
- **South African Rand (ZAR)** — freely traded but highly volatile; sensitive to commodity prices and EM sentiment

### Fixed & Pegged Currencies

Countries heavily involved in international trade may opt to minimize currency volatility by fixing or pegging their currency to another, trading monetary policy independence for exchange rate stability.

|Type|Example|Pegged To|
|---|---|---|
|Fixed|Hong Kong Dollar (HKD)|USD|
|Pegged (with band)|Danish Krone (DKK)|EUR|
|Currency board|Bulgarian Lev (BGN)|EUR|

> **Note:** Fixed/pegged regimes require significant FX reserves to defend the peg. If reserves are depleted, the peg can break — as seen in the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis and the 1992 Black Wednesday (GBP/ERM).

## Monetary Policy & Currency Value

Central banks are the primary institutions responsible for managing a currency's value and supply.

**Key tools:**

- **Interest rates** — higher rates attract foreign capital, strengthening the currency
- **Open market operations** — buying/selling government bonds to influence money supply
- **FX intervention** — directly buying or selling currency in the market to influence the exchange rate
- **Quantitative easing (QE)** — expanding the money supply, typically weakening the currency

**Key institutions:** Federal Reserve (Fed), European Central Bank (ECB), Bank of England (BoE), Bank of Japan (BoJ), People's Bank of China (PBOC)

## The FX Market

The foreign exchange (FX) market is where currencies of different countries are bought and sold. It is the largest and most liquid financial market in the world.

- **Daily volume:** ~$7.5 trillion (as of 2022 BIS Triennial Survey)
- Operates 24 hours a day, 5 days a week across global financial centers (Sydney → Tokyo → London → New York)
- Decentralized — no single exchange; trades occur over-the-counter (OTC)

### Key FX Concepts

- **Spot rate** — the current exchange rate for immediate delivery
- **Forward rate** — a locked-in exchange rate for a transaction at a future date
- **Pip** — the smallest standard price increment in an FX quote (usually 0.0001)
- **Spread** — the difference between the buy (ask) and sell (bid) price
- **Carry trade** — borrowing in a low-interest-rate currency to invest in a higher-yielding one

## Who Trades in FX Markets?

### Institutional Investors

|Participant|Motivation|
|---|---|
|**Equity investors**|Must exchange currency when buying/selling foreign equities|
|**Fixed income investors**|Treat FX as its own asset class; manage currency exposure on foreign bonds|
|**Commodity traders**|Hedge commodity exposure (e.g. oil is priced in USD, so non-US producers hedge via FX)|
|**Global macro funds**|Take directional views on currencies as part of a broad cross-asset thesis|

### Corporations

- **Transaction exposure** — minimize FX risk on known future cash flows (e.g. a UK firm invoicing in USD hedges GBP/USD)
- **Translation exposure** — manage the accounting impact of converting foreign subsidiary earnings back to the home currency
- **Economic exposure** — longer-term strategic risk that exchange rate shifts affect competitiveness

### Central Banks & Governments

Intervene to stabilize exchange rates, manage reserves, or signal policy direction.

### Retail Investors & Speculators

Participate via brokers and CFD platforms. Represent a small fraction of overall volume but have grown with the rise of retail trading platforms.

## Key Risks in FX

- **Exchange rate risk** — adverse currency moves reduce returns on foreign assets
- **Convertibility risk** — some currencies cannot be freely exchanged (capital controls)
- **Political/sovereign risk** — government actions (sanctions, defaults) can impair a currency
- **Liquidity risk** — emerging market currencies can become illiquid during stress events
